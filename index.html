☁ Cloud Catalog

Explore all 10 major cloud types, organized by altitude. Learn to identify what's happening in the sky above you.

High Altitude Clouds above 20,000 ft (6,000 m)

☁

Cirrus

20,000–40,000 ft (6,000–12,000 m)

Thin, wispy strands of ice crystals blown by high-altitude winds. Often the first sign of an approaching weather front. They appear bright white and hair-like against a blue sky.

☁

Cirrocumulus

20,000–40,000 ft (6,000–12,000 m)

Small, white puffs arranged in rows or ripples, resembling fish scales. Composed entirely of ice crystals. Often called a 'mackerel sky' due to its pattern.

☁

Cirrostratus

20,000–40,000 ft (6,000–12,000 m)

A thin, transparent sheet of ice crystals that covers much of the sky. Causes a halo effect around the sun or moon. Usually indicates rain or snow within 12–24 hours.

Mid Altitude Clouds 6,500–20,000 ft (2,000–6,000 m)

☁

Altocumulus

6,500–20,000 ft (2,000–6,000 m)

Gray or white patches, sheets, or waves of cloud, larger than cirrocumulus puffs. Composed mainly of water droplets. Often seen on warm, humid mornings before afternoon thunderstorms.

☁

Altostratus

6,500–20,000 ft (2,000–6,000 m)

A uniform, gray or blue-gray sheet that covers the sky. Thick enough to block the sun, producing a dull, overcast look. Associated with prolonged rain or snow.

☁

Nimbostratus

6,500–20,000 ft (2,000–6,000 m)

A dark, thick, featureless cloud layer that produces continuous moderate-to-heavy precipitation. The cloud layer is so thick that sunlight rarely penetrates it.

Low Altitude Clouds 0–6,500 ft (0–2,000 m)

☁

Stratus

0–6,500 ft (0–2,000 m)

A low, uniform, gray layer that resembles fog. Produces drizzle or light rain. When it forms at ground level it is called fog. Associated with dull, grey days with little sunshine.

☁

Stratocumulus

0–6,500 ft (0–2,000 m)

Low, lumpy patches or rolls of cloud, often in groups. The most common cloud type worldwide. Rarely produces significant rainfall. Appears in shades of white and gray.

☁

Cumulus

1,600–6,500 ft (500–2,000 m)

Fluffy, white clouds with flat bases and dome-shaped tops. The classic 'fair weather' cloud when small, but can grow into storm clouds. Forms from rising thermal currents.

☁

Cumulonimbus

1,600–40,000+ ft (500–12,000+ m)

Massive thunderstorm cloud that extends from low altitudes to the tropopause. Produces heavy rain, hail, lightning, and tornadoes. Characterized by an anvil-shaped top.

Cloud classifications based on the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) International Cloud Atlas.
